Backyard Ultra
Crewing Tips

I've crewed at two backyard ultras. Here are 11 things I wish I'd known before my first event.

Your athlete will run each loop in about 50 minutes (average at most backyards). That gives you ten minutes or less to crew for them.

In that time, you've got to be precise. The mistakes you make could cost them the race: under- or over-fueling, gear malfunction, arriving late to the corral.

Our first race, we were a crew of three. And we were sloppy. We'd chill in the tent while our runner crossed the finish line. We didn't track any macros. And we asked them to make way too many decisions.

We kept a trail journal where we wrote down all of our mistakes, thoughts, and feelings loop by loop. It was crucial in helping us improve for our next race, the BPN Go One More.

In Austin, our runner's nutrition was dialed in. We felt more confident. We did well. One failure point was rest. We didn't take care of ourselves as much as we should have. We were heavily sleep deprived, and our crewing got sloppy in the later rounds.

These events are brutal, for both athlete and crew. Mistakes are to be expected. But with every race, we get a little better. Here are 11 lessons I'll carry into the next one.

1. Be ready when they arrive

Don't wait in the tent. When your runner crosses the finish line, you should already be there, hands full, ready to execute. Every second in the ten-minute window counts, and losing thirty seconds because you were sitting down is thirty seconds your athlete doesn't get to rest.

Know exactly where you'll stand at the finish, where the gear is staged, and who on your crew handles what. Rehearse it. Make it automatic.

2. Repackage food

Before the race, prepare single portions of candy, fruits, chips, and other snacks in small ziplock bags. Label each bag with its carb, calorie, and sodium amounts.

This removes decision-making at the aid station. You reach for a bag, hand it over, and your runner grabs it knowing exactly what they're getting. No fumbling, no reading labels, no guessing. It also makes tracking macros across loops much easier.

3. Don't ask. Tell.

Your athlete will be cognitively depleted long before their body gives out. The last thing they need is more decisions. So don't ask "What do you want to eat?" or "Do you want to sleep next loop?" Take initiative.

Study their preferences before the race. Build a loop-by-loop plan. Then execute it. If something needs to change, make the call yourself and tell them what's happening. Their job is to run. Your job is everything else.

4. Two minutes of 'sleep' is better than none

At night, if your athlete has time, have them lie down and close their eyes, even if it's only for a couple of minutes. A face mask, earplugs, and noise-canceling headphones can make a real difference in a loud, busy camp.

Even very short rest breaks can reduce cognitive fatigue and help your athlete push further into the night. Build this into your loop routine so it becomes a habit rather than a scramble.

5. Some food every loop

Your runner needs to eat and drink sufficiently during every single loop, not just when they feel hungry. Hunger signals become unreliable after extended effort. Underfueling or dehydration will cost them the race before their body would have otherwise quit.

Make sure there's always gas in the tank. Even a small amount of calories and fluid every loop is better than a big meal every few loops.

6. Real food every few hours

Snacks sustain. Real food restores. Prepare solid, warm meals every four to five hours: think breakfast, lunch, and dinner on repeat. Eggs, rice, soup, pasta, whatever your athlete can stomach.

Warm food prevents the stomach problems that often end races early, and it gives your runner something to actually look forward to. A hot meal at 3 am is a powerful mental boost.

7. Document everything

Use a race journal to track what happens loop by loop: what your runner ate, how they looked, what gear they changed, how their mood shifted. Include your own observations and gut feelings too.

After the race, study it. The patterns you find will tell you more than any post-race debrief. It's the single best thing you can do to improve your crewing for the next event.

8. Take care of yourself

You are useless to your runner if you're sleep deprived. Your thinking needs to be sharp, your emotions stable, and your movements precise. Sloppy crewing in the late loops comes from exhausted crew, not just exhausted athletes.

Build a crew rotation so everyone gets rest. Treat your own recovery with the same seriousness you treat your athlete's.

9. Let them see and hear you

Be there at every starting line, every finish line, and every midway point the race allows. Call out their name. Make noise. Your runner might not acknowledge you, and that's fine. They'll know you're there. And it will help.

Presence is its own kind of fuel. Especially in the dark, when the field has thinned out and it's just them and the clock.

10. Don't get attached to plan A

What can go wrong will go wrong. Your runner might get sick. Their gear might fail. A storm might blow your tent over. Race logistics might change. Expect disruption, and build contingency into everything.

The crews who handle chaos well aren't the ones who planned more. They're the ones who stayed calm and adapted faster.

11. Bring them back

Late in the race, your athlete will be in real pain. They may hallucinate. They will want out. This is when crewing matters most.

Don't try to fix everything or talk them out of how they feel. Remind them who they are and why they started. Keep it simple, keep it grounded, keep it honest. They signed up for this. Your job is to help them remember that.
